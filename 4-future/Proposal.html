Research Collaboration Proposal

Securing Autonomous AI Agents: Supply-Chain Screening, Credential Revocation, and Verifiable Accountability

1. Why now, and why this problem

An agent's blast radius is determined by the tools it can call, not by its weights. In eighteen months the ecosystem has moved from agents with a few hand-written tools to agents that install third-party capabilities — MCP servers, skills, plugins — from public registries with essentially no review.

Three findings define the gap:

  • Prevalence. Large-scale audits of public agent-skill registries report that roughly a quarter of scanned skills carry at least one security vulnerability, spanning more than a dozen distinct patterns.
  • Detector fragility. In a controlled evaluation of eight open-source skill scanners, a single malicious skill evaded all eight using encoding, homoglyph substitution, paraphrase and bundled-code techniques.
  • Tooling mismatch. Signature-based malware infrastructure does not recognise agent-targeted payloads; catalogues of thousands of malicious agent-facing tools show conventional scanning services missing the majority.

Underneath the supply-chain problem sits an identity problem. A perfectly screened tool still becomes dangerous when the agent invoking it holds a long-lived, broadly-scoped credential that nobody can revoke quickly. Standards bodies have started saying this plainly: agents are commonly deployed as generic service accounts, with no dedicated identity, authorization or accountability controls.

Screening, revocation and accountability are usually pursued as separate literatures. I believe they are one control loop — decide what an agent may install, revoke it quickly when that decision was wrong, and prove afterwards what it actually did. Directions 1 to 3 below are that loop.

The core is already built. The sandbox-and-verify auditing agent that Direction 1 retargets is not hypothetical: it exists today as a working, tested system — a four-stage pipeline (comprehend, plan, classify, self-correct) behind a read-only sandbox, with a hash-chained audit trail, evaluated on 121 real annotated contracts from the SmartBugs curated corpus. I report its result honestly, including where it currently trails a rule-based baseline; that measured baseline is exactly what makes the adversarial-screening and public-benchmark work below the valuable next step rather than a restatement. The architecture and the exploit-corpus schema are in the attached technical appendix, and I can share the repository before we speak.

3. Direction 1 — Agent-skill and MCP supply-chain screening

Recommended starting point.

What we would build. A screening system that survives adversarial evasion, and a labelled public benchmark that makes competing detectors comparable for the first time.

  • Manifest analysis — permission and verb extraction, capability diffing across versions to detect privilege creep between releases of the same skill.
  • Prompt-layer analysis — implicit tool poisoning, instruction smuggling in descriptions and docstrings, and the encoding and homoglyph families that defeated existing scanners.
  • Behavioural analysis — execute each skill in a read-only sandbox, capture actual syscall, network and tool egress, and diff observed behaviour against declared capability. This is the sandbox-and-verify structure from my contract auditing work, retargeted.
  • Benchmark — a versioned corpus of benign and malicious skills with provenance and a held-out split, released publicly.
  • Leaderboard website — a public, live site that hosts the benchmark, ranks submitted detectors on the held-out split, and gives the corpus a citable home. This is the artifact other groups return to, and the one that turns a dataset into a reference.

How it would run.

MonthsWork

1–2Reproduce published evasion techniques against existing open-source scanners. This baseline comparison is a publishable result in itself and costs almost nothing additional.

Why I recommend it. It is the area with the most attention and the least settled tooling, which means a well-built benchmark becomes the reference other groups cite rather than one paper among many. It also produces a defensible patent and an adoptable artifact from the same nine months of work, and it creates the corpus that Direction 4 would otherwise have to build from scratch.

From you I would ask scoping judgement on the threat taxonomy, review of the evaluation design, your name on the outputs, and — if you would like it — the paper, led from your side. No compute or data purchase is required: the corpus is drawn from public registries, and I can begin immediately.

4. Direction 2 — Credential revocation for autonomous agents

Strongest alternative; the closest fit to your own published work.

What we would build. Your smart-contract-based key revocation scheme for IoT/WoT environments solves a problem that agent infrastructure is about to meet in a harder form. A revoked IoT device is passive. A revoked agent is not: it may be mid-task, holding cached capability tokens, open tool sessions, and delegations it has already issued to sub-agents. Revoking it correctly means revoking a tree, with a propagation guarantee, fast enough to matter.

  • Per-agent identity distinct from the human principal, with delegation chains recording who authorized what, under which scope, and for how long.
  • Task-scoped capability tokens that expire on task completion rather than on a fixed calendar, behind a deny-by-default tool gate.
  • Cascading revocation — revoking a parent invalidates the delegations it issued, with a stated and measured propagation guarantee.
  • A Solidity revocation registry, plus the agent-side enforcement library that actually honours it.

How it would run.

MonthsWork
1Formalise the delegation-tree threat model; specify cascading revocation semantics and the propagation guarantee to be proven.
2Revocation registry contract and agent-side enforcement library.
3Provisional patent filing on cascading revocation with propagation guarantee, co-invented.
4Reference implementation of task-scoped capability tokens behind a deny-by-default gate.
5Measurement: revocation propagation latency and on-chain cost under load.
6Public release and joint paper at a venue of your choosing.

Why it is compelling. This is the direction where you are already the domain expert, which makes it the lowest-risk collaboration of the five and the one most likely to produce work you would want to keep building on. The measurements are the contribution — a revocation protocol that nobody has benchmarked under load is a design note, not a result.

5. Direction 3 — Tamper-evident audit trail for regulated agents

Recommended as the joint follow-on once Direction 1 or 2 is running.

What we would build. An append-only, hash-chained record of what an agent actually did, with digests anchored on-chain so that a party who does not trust the operator can still verify the log was not rewritten after an incident. Digests only — never payloads, so no client or patient data reaches a public ledger.

  • Hash-chained logging library with Merkle batching, so anchoring cost stays flat as event volume grows.
  • An independent verifier that runs without our infrastructure. This is the actual product: a log only anyone can check is a log nobody need trust us about.
  • A cost model against real retention horizons — regulated advice and trading records run to roughly seven years — demonstrating the curve is linear and bounded.
  • A published format specification and a response to the open consultation on securing AI agent systems.

Why it matters now. Of the five, this has the clearest regulatory pull. It maps onto named, dated compliance obligations rather than a research trend, and auditors have begun asking for runtime evidence of what an agent did rather than configuration screenshots. It depends on the trace format that Direction 1 or 2 will have established, which is why I propose it second rather than first.

6. Direction 4 — Explainable runtime intrusion detection for agents

Strong fit with your existing line; best pursued attached to Direction 1.

What we would build. Anomaly detection over agent tool-call traces, with an explanation layer that tells a reviewer why a particular call was flagged. Your ensemble-based intrusion detection work for smart city protection, and your explainable machine learning framework in Scientific Reports, are both directly applicable — the detection substrate transfers, and the explanation requirement is the same one clinicians and analysts impose.

  • Instrument tool-call traces; build normal-behaviour baselines per agent role.
  • Ensemble detector with an explanation layer over each flagged call.
  • Evaluation against a labelled corpus of genuinely malicious agent behaviour.
An honest note on sequencing. This direction needs a labelled corpus of real malicious agent behaviour to be evaluated credibly, and no good public one exists yet — which is precisely what Direction 1 produces. Run standalone, the detector would have to be evaluated on synthetic data, and reviewers discount that heavily. Run after or alongside Direction 1, it inherits a defensible evaluation set and becomes a considerably stronger result. I would therefore propose it as a second phase rather than an entry point, unless you have access to a labelled trace corpus I am not aware of — in which case it becomes immediately viable and I would be glad to start here.

7. Direction 5 — Clinical agent safety benchmark

Viable and well-matched to your healthcare record; a different problem domain.

What we would build. A safety benchmark for agents acting on clinical data and workflows — what a clinical agent must refuse, what it must escalate, and what it must never do autonomously. Your explainable machine learning work on hospital length-of-stay prediction and my work with Prof. Liantao Ma at Peking University MedX and Prof. Junyi Gao at the University of Edinburgh's Centre for Medical Informatics, on ICU physiological time-series and MIMIC-IV and VitalDB pipelines, give this a credible foundation on both sides.

  • Task suite covering refusal, escalation and autonomy boundaries for clinical agents.
  • Governance note on data handling and de-identification.
  • Release through an established dataset host with a citable identifier.
An honest note on scope. This is real work and I would enjoy it, but it addresses clinical safety rather than the agent security questions in Directions 1 to 3, and it carries data governance overhead that the others do not. If your priority is the security research path you described, I would hold this one. If you have an active clinical collaboration where it would land well, it becomes considerably more attractive and I am ready to contribute.

8. Sequencing, outputs, and one constraint

A single constraint that shapes every timeline. Public disclosure ahead of a provisional patent filing forecloses protection. Wherever a direction produces both a filing and an open release, the filing must come first. I have placed each filing gate at month three to four, before the corresponding public release. This protects joint intellectual property, and it is the one part of the schedule I would ask not to compress.
